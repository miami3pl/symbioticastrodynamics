Large-Scale Structures Laboratory

Monopart Research at Full Scale

Large structures are usually studied in pieces. A coupon, a section, a subscale model, and then a careful similitude argument that carries the result up to full size.

That method is often the right one. It is also the place where joints go missing, because a coupon has none and the finished article has hundreds. We acquired the machine that lets a structure be studied whole instead. The JOBS LinX 6-L is a gantry mill built by JOBS S.p.A. of Italy, the large-part brand of the EMCO Group. Its axes travel 86.6 by 126 by 49.2 inches over a table measuring 98 by 158 inches. It is presently in storage, pending installation and commissioning.

Eight feet two inches by thirteen feet two inches. One piece. No joints.

That table is larger than three sheets of plywood laid edge to edge, and carries roughly the floor area of a small bedroom. Once installed, it will hold a single piece of metal that size flat, to be cut whole.

The Monopart Idea

What We Mean by Monopart

We use monopart for a large structural component cut from a single billet or plate: one piece where an assembly would use many. A rib, a bulkhead, a frame. Every joint in an assembly is an interface that has to be designed, analyzed, fastened and inspected. A monopart has none of them, because there is nothing to join.

Why the Envelope Is the Ceiling

The largest structure you can study in one piece is the largest one the machine can hold. Below that limit, a design question is answered directly on the real article. Above it, the specimen is divided into sections and the joints become part of the experiment whether you wanted them there or not.

One Article at a Time

This is a research instrument, not a rate. The intent is singular: one article cut, instrumented, tested, and recorded for the engineering and patent file. The value is in what a single full-size article tells us, not in how many of them exist.

What It Will Bring Within Reach

Once commissioned: aluminum plate structures up to 8 by 13 feet, single-piece mold surfaces for hand wet layup test articles, and structural specimens studied at the size they will actually be. A mold cut in one piece removes the seam a split mold would print into every specimen laid up on it.

Prototype Development Laboratory

The Closed Loop

Cut the electrode. Burn the cavity. Hold the dielectric clean. Then measure what you actually got.

In prototype research the delay is rarely the cutting. It is the wait between steps, and a study that waits three weeks for a cavity is a study that stops. Holding the steps together is what lets a question get asked twice in the same month, and asking it twice is the difference between an observation and a result.

The chain below is owned and catalogued. It is in storage now, with installation and commissioning running through 2026, and what follows describes each machine and its place in the loop rather than work being done today. The turning, measurement and additive capability in the second group is in service now.

The Electrode Loop

High-Speed Bridge Milling

Two Röders RFM-1000/2 bridge mills, 30,000 RPM spindles, linear-motor axes, Röders RMS6 control. Their first role in the loop is milling the graphite electrodes the sinker EDMs burn with. The same spindle is rated to cut hardened steel directly and to finish to a mirror without hand polishing, which is what puts optical-quality surfaces, lens molds and reflectors within reach of a one-off study.

Sinker EDM

Three Makino sinker EDMs. The EDNC-156 carries a C-axis, so the electrode is driven in programmed orbital motion rather than plunged straight down, with 59 inches of X travel over a 39 by 79 inch table. Beside it, an EDNC-106W and an EDNC-85. This is how geometry no cutter can reach gets put into hardened steel.

Clean Dielectric

A Transor ultra-fine filtration system, 3,000 gallons, one-micron filtration, 25,000-hour filter life, self-cleaning backflush, sized to serve all three sinkers from one loop. Dielectric condition is an experimental variable in EDM work, not a housekeeping detail. One system means all three machines will answer to one standard, which is what makes a burn on one comparable to a burn on another.

Forming Studies

A Clearing 250-ton tryout press, 5 by 10 foot bed, 120 inches of daylight. Its role is the fourth step: coining and deep-draw studies against geometry cut by the first three machines. It is also the one machine the acquisition record flags for site work before first use. Foundation, lifting access and power precede it, and that work is scoped rather than assumed.

How a Program Runs

01 Concept and Analysis

We start from the physics the problem actually obeys: governing parameters, the regime the design will live in, what is settled by first principles and what will only be settled by a test. The list of unknowns is written down before anything is drawn.

02 Design

Geometry, tolerances, materials and the instrumentation plan drawn together. Sensor access, mounting and balance interfaces are designed at the same time as the part they attach to, because a specimen that cannot be measured has told you nothing.

03 Build One

One article, built to be tested. Five-axis turn-mill and CNC turning, bonded assembly, hand wet-layup composite structure, and polymer additive for fast geometric iteration. Machines still in commissioning are named as such before a schedule is agreed.

04 Instrument It

RF and electromagnetic instrumentation on electrical articles, and the flow laboratory's own measurement chain on fluid and rotating articles. Channels, ranges and sampling are chosen for the question being asked, and the conditions of every run are recorded with it.

05 Measure

Controlled runs against a stated baseline, with the instrument, the range and the conditions written down beside the number. Data comes back with the conditions each run was taken under, and the runs that failed come back too.

06 Iterate

One variable at a time, so an improvement can be told apart from a coincidence. Revisions are cut and retested on the same instrumentation and against the same baseline, and the record of everything tried travels with the article.

Origin: White & Grey

Where discovery began

The founder's daily interactions with two cockatiels—White and Grey—became an unexpected window into flow dynamics that textbooks don't capture.

Birds don't read aerodynamics papers. They simply fly. And in watching them maneuver through confined spaces, hover during play, and adjust wingtip geometry mid-flight, patterns emerged that contradicted conventional fixed-wing assumptions.

Grey

Explosive. Aggressive. Physics as suggestion.

Split-second transitions from climb to dive to hover—not planned, not gradual, but instantaneous reversals that seem to violate inertia itself. Grey flies like physics is a suggestion, not a law. Primary feathers splay into configurations no aircraft designer would approve. Efficiency should collapse. It increases.

White

Tranquil. Controlled. Perfect economy.

Every movement deliberate, every correction minimal. Where Grey attacks the air, White negotiates with it. Smooth ascents. Patient hovers. Descents so controlled they appear suspended. White demonstrates that efficiency isn't about power—it's about knowing exactly how much force is necessary and applying precisely that.

Two birds. Two philosophies. Both achieving what engineered systems cannot.

The Pattern Studies

Smoke visualization around White and Grey revealed structures we weren't expecting to find.

What the textbooks predict

  • Wingtip vortices forming at lift termination points
  • Downwash proportional to lift coefficient
  • Turbulent wake behind the body
  • Standard induced drag relationships

What we observed

  • Vortex systems of staggering complexity and precision
  • Phase-locked vortex relay systems
  • Standing vortex patterns of impossible stability
  • Organized air cycling with sub-threshold variation

A 90-gram bird executes fluid mechanics we cannot replicate, cannot fully model, and—if we're honest—do not fully understand.

We have computational fluid dynamics packages that cost more than houses. We have wind tunnels. We have centuries of aerodynamic theory. And a cockatiel hovering over a kitchen table demonstrates vortex control that exceeds anything in the literature.
